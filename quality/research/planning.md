# Evidence on planning the content before the medium: write the teaching article first, then adapt it

Scope. mr-cast should first write a full, readable explanatory article (a pure teaching text that would serve equally for a talk, a written page, or a video), get it critiqued by a cheap simulated reader, and only then adapt it into narration and visuals. This report collects evidence on that pipeline. It builds on teaching.md (Mayer principles, worked examples, concreteness fading, refutation, narrative, expertise reversal) and reviewing.md (reviewer question order); those findings are not repeated, only referenced.

## Per-source findings

Format: claim; evidence strength; concrete rule for mr-cast.

### A. Instructional design: objectives and content before materials

1. Backward design (Wiggins and McTighe, Understanding by Design; not opened). Claim: decide desired understandings, then what evidence would show them, then plan instruction. Evidence: I searched for outcome studies and found only overviews, practitioner guides and a literature review with no outcome data; a teaching wiki cited a Bowen 2017 review and a Kelting-Gibson 2005 comparison that I could not confirm. Strength: practice wisdom with face validity; no verified causal evidence. Rule: the article starts from a stated takeaway and the questions that would prove the reader got it, written before any prose. The questions double as the critique test (section G).

2. Objectives given to learners (Klauer 1984, snippet; Hamilton 1985, via blog). Claim: stating objectives or questions before a text improves learning of goal-relevant material, impedes learning of goal-irrelevant material, and helps slightly overall; objectives that name the information to be learned work, Mager-style behavioral detail adds nothing. Evidence: a meta-analysis of 23 reports and 52 comparisons (if the attribution is right), small effects. Note the seductive-details study in item 12 found that giving objectives made off-topic details more harmful. Strength: moderate, small effect. Rule: the takeaway list is a filter: every paragraph must serve a takeaway, and anything that does not is cut or moved to "left out", because objectives make readers ignore the rest anyway.

3. Teacher clarity (Hattie, d = 0.75, snippets only). Claim: communicating learning intentions and success criteria is among the larger effects in Visible Learning. Strength: weak as a causal estimate (Hattie's synthesis methods are widely criticized; I did not verify the number). Rule: none beyond item 2; do not cite this number.

4. Gagne's nine events (Li et al. 2025 meta-analysis, opened). Claim: lessons built on the nine events beat plain lectures. Evidence: 11 studies, 825 participants, health professions; pooled knowledge SMD 1.55 with I² = 95%, but RCTs alone gave SMD 0.43 (I² = 0%) and cohort studies 2.20; the authors note low quality and internal inconsistencies. Strength: weak to moderate; the honest number is the RCT one, a small-to-moderate benefit of a structured sequence over unstructured lecturing. Rule: use the sequence as a checklist for the article (gain attention, state the goal, recall prerequisites, present, guide with an example, elicit, give feedback, assess, aid transfer), not as evidence that any particular order is optimal.

5. ADDIE (not verified). A process model (analyze, design, develop, implement, evaluate) from practice. No outcome evidence sought or found. Rule: its useful idea is that analysis and design are separate, earlier phases from development; mr-cast's article is the design artifact and the video is development.

### B. Presenter and talk preparation

6. Reynolds, Presentation Zen (secondary summaries only). Claim: prepare away from the computer (paper, whiteboard, sticky notes), find the core message with "so what?" and an elevator test, because slideware pushes presenters into templates too early. Strength: expert practice, no study. Duarte (Resonate, slide:ology) and Made to Stick (simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, stories) are in the same category and were not verified in this session. Rule: the content stage must not have access to the visual vocabulary; give it no list of primitives, so it cannot plan around what is easy to draw.

7. Assertion-evidence slides (Alley and colleagues; authors' research page opened). Claims: (a) audiences shown sentence-headline plus visual-evidence slides understood and remembered more than audiences shown topic-plus-bullets slides with the same narration (Garner, Alley, Wolfe, Sawarynski 2013, IJEE 29(6), p < .01, delayed one-week quiz per a teaching-centre summary); (b) student presenters who built assertion-evidence slides understood their own content better, plausibly because writing each claim as a full sentence forces deeper thinking (Aippersbach, Alley, Garner 2013, ASEE). Evidence: a handful of studies by one group, single topic (MRI), sample sizes not seen, no independent replication found. Strength: weak to moderate. Rule: every section of the article opens with its claim as a full declarative sentence, not a topic label. That is a writing rule, and it later carries over cleanly to the adaptation (each segment states its assertion).

### C. Writing an explanation that teaches

8. Instructional explanations often fail (Wittwer and Renkl 2008, 2010; Schworm and Renkl 2002; secondary only). Claim: explanations frequently show little or no learning benefit; the 2010 meta-analysis of 21 studies and 28 comparisons found small benefits with high variability, larger for conceptual than procedural knowledge (secondary summary). Their framework, from memory: adapt the explanation to what the learner already knows, focus on concepts and principles rather than surface steps, and make the learner do something with it. Schworm and Renkl's title says explanations attached to worked examples reduced learners' self-explanation. Strength: moderate that explanations are fragile; the guidelines are expert synthesis. Rule: the article must name its assumed reader and their prior knowledge, explain the principle behind each step (the "because"), target one specific likely misunderstanding, and leave the reader at least one point to work out (a question posed before its answer).

9. Worked examples, concreteness fading, refutation, narrative (see teaching.md items 2, 5, 10, 14). They apply to text at least as well as to video: almost all the underlying experiments used printed materials. Rule: one running example traced end to end, introduced before the general rule; the misconception stated, marked wrong, then replaced; causal "because/so" connectives.

10. Text cohesion depends on the reader (McNamara and Kintsch 1996, snippets). Claim: low-knowledge readers learn more from highly coherent text (explicit links, defined terms, no gaps); high-knowledge readers can do better with a less explicit text that makes them infer. Strength: moderate (several experiments by McNamara and colleagues; I saw only abstracts and later citations). This is the textual form of expertise reversal. Rule: for the default reader (a competent developer new to the concept), write high-cohesion prose: define each term at first use, make every inference explicit, never refer to something not yet introduced.

11. Less can be more (Mayer et al. 1996, design via secondary source, results from memory: summaries of captioned illustrations beat the full 600-word passage on recall of the explanation and on transfer). Also coherence principle (teaching.md item 1). Strength: moderate for the coherence effect, my memory for this specific study. Rule: the article is as short as the takeaways allow. Length is not a quality signal; a critique that asks for more content must name the takeaway it serves.

12. Seductive details (PMC8442593 opened, citing Rey 2012: retention d = 0.30, transfer d = 0.48, 39 studies, with 15 of 39 studies not supporting; Sundararajan and Adesope 2020: g = -0.37 for retention). The opened study (112 undergraduates, word counts matched) found the effect only when objectives were given, and argues earlier studies confounded interest with extra length. Strength: moderate but conditional. Rule: no interesting-but-irrelevant anecdotes or history in the article; when unsure, cut.

### D. Text versus narrated video, and adapting one into the other

13. Video versus other instruction (Noetel et al. 2021, Review of Educational Research, snippet: 105 randomized trials, 7,776 students; replacing teaching with video g = 0.28, adding video g = 0.80; a later preprint disputes it). Tarchi et al. 2021 (snippet, 247 undergraduates): text, video and subtitled video were similar on immediate comprehension; subtitled video hurt deep learning. Other single studies point both ways. Strength: moderate that video is not worse and not magic. Rule: the article is a legitimate deliverable on its own; the video's job is to add what text cannot (item 15), not to re-say the text.

14. Transient information (Leahy and Sweller 2011, 2016, snippets). Claim: spoken words vanish; for long or complex passages, written text beats narration, and the usual modality advantage for narration appears only for short segments. Strength: moderate (CLT experiments). Rule for adaptation: narration must be shorter per idea than the article's prose; long definitions, lists of conditions, or code that must be held in mind stay on screen as persistent text or code, not spoken only. Content that depends on rereading is a reason to keep a written companion.

15. Animation versus static graphics (Tversky et al. 2002, abstract extracted; Hoffler and Leutner 2007, snippet). Tversky: where animation looked better, "scrutiny reveals lack of equivalence between animated and static graphics in content or procedures; the animated graphics convey more information or involve interactivity"; animations fail the Apprehension Principle (too fast or complex to perceive) and continuous change is conceived as discrete steps. Hoffler and Leutner: overall d = 0.37 over 26 studies, larger when the animation is representational (not decorative) and for procedural-motor knowledge (d = 1.06); Berney and Betrancourt 2016 found g = 0.23 across 61 studies. Strength: moderate meta-analytic. Rule for adaptation: animate only what changes over time in the explanation (state transitions, data moving, a pointer advancing), in discrete steps matching the article's steps; everything else can be static.

16. Educational video guidelines (Brame 2016, opened; Guo et al. 2014, opened). Guo, 6.9 million sessions, four edX courses: median engagement at most 6 minutes regardless of length; videos over 9 minutes often watched less than halfway; videos made for online beat recorded lectures; and edX producers felt pre-production planning had the largest impact on engagement, with Khan-style tutorials requiring "more pre-production planning". Guo measures engagement time, not learning, and the planning claim is producer opinion plus one confounded course comparison. Brame synthesizes signaling, segmenting, weeding, modality matching, conversational style, interpolated questions (Szpunar 2013), learner control. Strength: Guo strong for engagement, weak for learning; Brame is a review of mostly lab evidence. Rule: the adaptation, not the article, is constrained to about 6 minutes; if the article cannot be told in that time, split it into parts rather than compressing below the takeaways.

17. Text-first pipelines for LLM-generated explainer videos (Code2Video 2025 and TheoremExplainAgent 2025, opened). Code2Video: a Planner writes an outline (sections with content summaries and examples, audience-aware) and then a storyboard, before a Coder writes Manim code and a vision-language Critic fixes layout. Removing the Planner caused the largest drop (with GPT-4.1, TeachQuiz 82.0 to 40.5); removing the Critic dropped it to 60.7. In a small human study (about 8 people per condition, 20 topics) quiz scores on Code2Video videos (80.3) matched human 3Blue1Brown videos (78.8), though humans ranked 3B1B first and the authors say human videos still lead on storytelling, sequencing and depth. TheoremExplainAgent: planner produces scene outline then per-scene visual plans; no planner ablation, only an agentless baseline that could not exceed 20 seconds; most videos had layout issues; in a 15-person study a flawed text explanation was judged correct by all, and 9 changed their judgment after watching the video. Strength: moderate for "plan first" in this exact task (one clean ablation, same lab's benchmark); weak for the human results (tiny samples). Rule: planning content before visuals is the single most valuable stage in the closest published systems. Note neither system writes a full prose article first; both go outline to storyboard, so the article-first step is mr-cast's own extension, supported by items 7, 18 and 19 rather than tested directly.

### E. LLM planning before writing

18. Plan-and-Write 2019, Re3 2022, DOC 2023, STORM 2024 (abstracts). Plan-and-Write: explicit storyline planning gave more diverse, coherent, on-topic stories by automatic and human evaluation; it compared static (plan everything first) and dynamic (interleaved) planning, and from memory static planning did better on coherence. Re3: plan, then draft with plan injected, rerank, edit: +14% absolute coherent plot and +20% premise relevance over direct generation. DOC: a more detailed, hierarchical outline plus a controller that keeps passages faithful to it: +22.5% plot coherence, +28.2% outline relevance, +20.7% interestingness over Re3. STORM: research and outline before writing Wikipedia-style articles, +25% absolute organized and +10% broad coverage; editors flagged source bias and over-association of unrelated facts. Narayan et al. 2023 (snippet): plans written as question-answer pairs help content selection and ordering; Fierro et al. 2024 (snippet) says plan-based models improve faithfulness and attribution. Strength: moderate to strong for long-form generation, consistently positive, but on stories and encyclopedic text, not teaching explanations, and evaluated by human preference rather than learning. Two failure modes are documented: the writer drifts from its plan (DOC exists because Re3's drafts drifted, hence "outline relevance" as a metric), and planners pull in loosely related material (STORM). Rule: the plan must be detailed (DOC's lesson), and adherence must be checked, not assumed. Express the plan's takeaways as questions with answers (Narayan), which makes adherence testable.

### F. Self-critique and refinement

19. Self-Refine 2023 (full text). About 20% absolute average gain over one-shot output across seven tasks with GPT-3.5/4. Gains shrink per round: constrained generation 29.0 to 40.3 to 46.7 to 49.7; code optimization 22.0 to 27.0 to 27.9 to 28.8. Math barely moved because ChatGPT's feedback said "everything looks good" 94% of the time. Of failures, 33% came from feedback pointing at the wrong place and 61% from feedback suggesting an inappropriate fix; only 6% from the refiner ignoring good feedback. Specific, actionable feedback beat generic feedback (sentiment reversal 43.2 vs 31.2). A weak model (Vicuna-13B) could not produce usable feedback at all. Strength: strong within its tasks (many tasks, human and automatic evaluation). Rule: most gain is in the first revision; feedback must be specific and located ("takeaway 2 is never answered; the example skips the partition step"), and the critic needs to be capable enough to find real problems. A cheap critic that just approves adds cost and nothing else.

20. Huang et al. 2024 (full text). Without external feedback, self-correction made reasoning worse: GPT-3.5 CommonSenseQA 75.8 to 41.8 after two rounds, GPT-4 GSM8K 95.5 to 89.0, HotpotQA 49.0 to 43.0; models more often changed right answers to wrong. Gains reported in earlier work came from oracle stopping or an underspecified first prompt; a better initial prompt on CommonGen-Hard reached 81.8 with no self-correction, above the 67.0 prior work got with it. Multi-agent debate did not beat self-consistency at equal sample counts. Strength: strong. Rule: do not loop "critique your own article" with the writer's own judgment as the only signal. The critique must carry an external, checkable signal (answers to fixed questions, section G). Put the effort into the first-draft instructions; revision is not a substitute for a clear brief.

21. Self-bias (Xu et al. 2024; Panickssery et al. 2024; abstracts). All six tested models favor their own outputs; self-refine improves fluency while amplifying this bias; models recognize their own text and self-recognition drives self-preference. Larger models and accurate external feedback reduce it. Strength: moderate to strong. Rule: the critic runs in a fresh context, preferably a different model or at least a different persona and instructions, and is never told the article is its own or asked whether the article is good.

22. Constitutional AI (full text section). Four critique-revision rounds; harmlessness scores rose monotonically with revisions but pure helpfulness scores fell; critiquing before revising helped small models, made little difference for large ones. Strength: strong for that setting. Rule: revision trades off qualities; each round must re-check every takeaway, not just the one criticized, and a round that lowers any score is reverted.

23. Reflexion (abstract). Verbal reflection stored across attempts raised HumanEval pass@1 to 91%, but the signal came from actual test execution. Rule: it supports critique loops only when there is a real test; here the test is the simulated reader's answers.

### G. Simulated readers and learners

24. Simulated-learner evaluation of materials (He-Yueya, Goodman, Brunskill 2024, full text). GPT-3.5 given a student persona predicted per-problem success after an instruction; it reproduced the expertise reversal and variability effects; worksheets optimized against these predictions were preferred by teachers (r = 0.661 with predicted scores, 56 teachers, 270 ratings). Not validated against real student learning; the authors say it does not replace testing with students. Their appendix: GPT-4 told to act as a novice solved about 40% of quiz problems after reading only the first six sentences of a transcript, so simulated students learn too fast. Strength: moderate as a cheap proxy, unvalidated for learning. Rule: never let the simulated reader use its own knowledge; constrain it to the article (item 27).

25. Generative Students (Lu and Wang 2024, abstract). 45 GPT-4 personas defined by which knowledge components they had mastered, were confused about, or had not shown; their answer patterns on 20 questions correlated highly with real students and flagged the same hard items; an instructor improved questions using the signals (not shown to improve real learning). Strength: moderate, small. Rule: define the target reader by explicit knowledge state (knows: arrays, loops, recursion; does not know: partitioning; holds misconception: pivot must be the median), not by a vague label like "beginner".

26. Validity warnings (Scarlatos et al. 2026; arXiv 2601.05473; abstracts). Prompted student simulators perform poorly against real tutoring data; fine-tuning helps but is still limited. The "competence paradox": capable LLMs asked to play partial learners make unrealistic errors and learn unrealistically. Strength: moderate. Rule: treat the simulated reader as a detector of gaps and ambiguities in the text, not as a prediction of human learning gain. Do not report its scores as learning outcomes.

27. TeachQuiz (Code2Video, full text). Score = accuracy answering 10 multiple-choice questions from the material alone minus accuracy with the same knowledge blocked by prompt ("answer INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE if blocked knowledge is needed"), so it measures what the material contributed. TeachQuiz correlated with aesthetics (r = 0.971 across systems) and the small human quiz roughly tracked it. Limits: prompt-based unlearning may leak, and the same model judged and critiqued. Strength: moderate; the best available operational design for a cheap test. Rule: test the article by baseline-subtracted question answering: the reader answers the fixed questions once without the article (with knowledge blocked) and once with only the article; credit is the difference, and any question answerable without the article is a weak question.

28. Subjective ratings do not predict usefulness (Hase and Bansal 2020, abstract). In human simulation tests, people's ratings of explanations did not predict whether the explanations helped them predict model behavior. Saha et al. 2023: a teacher LLM that models its student intervenes better, and misaligned explanations can drive a student to chance. Strength: moderate. Rule: the critic is asked to do the reader's task (answer, predict, trace the example), not to rate clarity. Report which questions failed and where the reader got stuck; "is it clear? 8/10" is not a signal.

29. QA-based and help-based text metrics (QAGS 2020, BLANC 2020, abstracts). QAGS: asking the same questions of a summary and its source and comparing answers tracks human judgments of factual consistency better than other automatic metrics. BLANC: summary quality measured as how much the summary helps a model do a task on the document, about as well correlated with humans as ROUGE without references. Strength: moderate. Rule: the same QA comparison checks the adaptation: ask the takeaway questions of the narration script and compare with answers from the article; a mismatch is a faithfulness error.

## Where evidence conflicts or is thin

- Planning objectives first is near-universal practice in instructional design and presentation craft, but I found no controlled study showing that planning content first, rather than designing materials directly, improves learning. Gagne's RCT-only estimate (SMD 0.43, health education) and Klauer's small effect are the closest, and they test structure or stated objectives, not planning order. The strongest causal evidence for plan-first comes from LLM generation (DOC, Re3, STORM, Code2Video's ablation), measured by human preference or simulated quizzes.
- Critique loops: Self-Refine says they help (about 20%), Huang says they hurt without external feedback, Constitutional AI shows they trade one quality for another. Reconciled: they help when feedback is specific and grounded in a check the model cannot fake, mostly in the first round, and hurt when the model judges itself.
- Simulated learners: He-Yueya and Lu and Wang show useful correlations; Scarlatos and the competence-paradox paper show prompted simulators are poor models of real learners; He-Yueya's own appendix shows they learn too fast. No study validated simulated-reader scores against real learning from an explanation. The TeachQuiz design (block knowledge, subtract baseline) addresses the main leak but is itself prompt-based.
- Text versus video: no consistent winner. Video's advantage, where it exists, is for change over time and procedures (Hoffler and Leutner), and Tversky warns many such advantages came from non-equivalent content. Transient information favors text for long or complex passages.
- Length: Guo's 6-minute guidance is about engagement, not learning. For the article, coherence and seductive-details work argues short; the article's length should follow its takeaways.
- Article-first, specifically: no study tests writing a complete prose explanation before adapting it to video. Support is indirect: assertion-evidence presenters understood their content better by writing claims as sentences (one group, small), plans that are more detailed keep generation more faithful (DOC), and text is where nearly all the explanation research was done.

## On keeping the content stage separate from the medium

What the evidence says about separation:
- Practice sources (Reynolds, edX producers in Guo, Khan-style tutorials) say planning away from the authoring tool produces better material, because tools push toward their own templates. No experiment isolates this.
- Code2Video's ablation is the closest test: when the planner is removed and the model goes straight to visual code, teaching scores roughly halve. The plan stage there is already medium-aware (it plans visual assets), so it does not prove a medium-free plan is better than a medium-aware one.
- Tversky's equivalence point implies the reverse risk too: if the visuals add or drop content relative to the article, any judgment of "the video teaches better" is confounded. Keeping the article as the fixed content spec makes the adaptation testable: same questions, same answers.
- DOC and Re3 show writers drift from plans unless something enforces adherence. The adaptation step will drift from the article unless checked.
So: the article is medium-free and is the content contract; the adaptation is a separate, later step whose only freedom is how to convey the article, checked by re-asking the article's questions.

## Rules for the article (content) stage, ranked

These are medium-free: they would apply equally to a lecture, a blog post, or a recording.

1. Start the plan with the reader and the takeaways, before any prose. Write: who the reader is, as an explicit knowledge state (what they know, what they do not, the misconception they likely hold); 2 to 4 takeaways, each a full sentence; and for each takeaway 1 to 3 questions with answers that a reader who got it could answer and one who did not could not, including at least one transfer question on a new input. (Backward design, Klauer, Generative Students, Narayan blueprints; moderate.)
2. Choose one running example and trace it all the way through; introduce it before the general rule, then state the rule, then say where it stops applying. (Worked examples, concreteness fading; strong in teaching.md.)
3. Order the ideas causally, each depending only on what came before; define each term at first use; connect steps with "because" and "so". (McNamara and Kintsch cohesion, Willingham narrative; moderate.)
4. Open each section with its claim as a complete sentence, not a topic label. (Assertion-evidence; weak to moderate.)
5. Name the one misconception, state it plainly, say why it fails on the running example, then give the correct model. (Refutation text, Wittwer and Renkl; moderate.)
6. Pose at least one question to the reader before answering it. (Self-explanation, Schworm and Renkl; moderate.)
7. Write a "left out" list: what was deliberately excluded and why. Every remaining paragraph must serve a takeaway; cut anecdotes, history and side facts. (Coherence, seductive details, Klauer's goal-irrelevant finding, STORM's over-association; moderate.)
8. Keep it as short as the takeaways allow; length is not quality. (Mayer 1996, coherence; moderate.)
9. Write the full article from the plan in one pass, with all of the above in the brief; invest in that brief rather than counting on revision. (Huang et al.; strong.)

## Rules for critiquing the article, and how many rounds

1. The critic is a separate call with a fresh context, preferably a different (cheaper is fine if it is competent at instruction following) model, playing the reader defined in rule 1 above. It never sees the plan's answers, and is never asked whether the article is good. (Self-bias, Panickssery; Self-Refine's Vicuna result for the competence floor.)
2. Test by task, not rating: (a) answer the plan's questions with knowledge outside the article blocked, once without the article and once with only the article; (b) trace the running example to its end and say where it got stuck; (c) list every term or step it had to guess. Score is answers right with the article minus answers right without; questions answerable without the article get rewritten. (TeachQuiz, Hase and Bansal, He-Yueya; moderate.)
3. Feedback to the writer is specific and located: the failed question, the sentence where the reader went wrong, the undefined term. No generic advice. The critic may not request new content unless it names the takeaway it serves. (Self-Refine specific vs generic feedback; Mayer coherence.)
4. At most 2 revision rounds, stopping early when all questions pass. Most gain is in round 1 (Self-Refine's per-round numbers); later rounds risk trading qualities (Constitutional AI) and amplifying self-bias (Xu). After each revision re-run the whole question set, and keep the previous version if any question that passed now fails.
5. Treat the critic's output as a gap detector, not a learning prediction. It reads too fast and knows too much (competence paradox, He-Yueya appendix); a pass is necessary, not sufficient. Periodically spot-check with a human reader.

## Rules for the later adaptation step (article to narration and visuals)

This is the only stage that may think about scenes, primitives or screens.

1. The article is the content contract. The adaptation may condense wording and reorder only within a section; it may not add claims, examples or facts, and may not drop a takeaway, the running example, or the misconception. (DOC adherence, Tversky equivalence.)
2. Check faithfulness by asking the plan's questions of the narration script (and on-screen labels) and comparing with answers from the article; any question the script cannot answer, or answers differently, is a defect. (QAGS, TeachQuiz.)
3. Narration per idea is shorter than the article's prose; content that must be held in mind (long conditions, code, definitions) stays visible as persistent text, not spoken only. (Transient information, modality, redundancy.)
4. Animate only what changes over time in the explanation, in discrete steps that match the article's steps; keep the rest static and undecorated. (Tversky, Hoffler and Leutner, Brame weeding.)
5. Keep each video around 6 minutes or less; if the article needs more, split it into parts instead of cutting takeaways. Keep the article available as a written companion. (Guo, Brame; Noetel for video as supplement.)
6. Visual critique (layout, overlap, legibility) is a separate check after the content is fixed. (Code2Video's Critic, TheoremExplainAgent's layout failures.)
